# The first human EE review — terminal-keyboard, 2026-08-15

The review packet asked our EE team one question: *would you let a non-engineer
use this unsupervised and order the boards it produces?* The first expert
answer came back on terminal-keyboard, and it is the most valuable artifact
this project has received. Verdict, translated:

> "Solid basic prototype — good enough that a non-specialist can produce a
> fairly complete PCB design. **Not manufacturable yet, because it would not
> work.**"

That sentence is the two-bar doctrine (`north-star.md`) spoken back to us by
someone who had never read it: `fab.ready` passed, and the board still fails
the bar that matters. Every finding below is recorded the way Dee directed:
**not as a terminal-keyboard fix, but as the generic defect class, verified by
measurement, with the structural fix that protects every future board.**

---

## Finding 1 — "Every switch in the key matrix is shorted"

**What the EE saw:** in the exported KiCad schematic, each key renders as a
2-pin switch symbol with a wire looping from pin 1 to pin 2 — the two contacts
tied together. As drawn, pressing the button does nothing. All 50 keys.

**What we measured:** the copper is (almost certainly) correct; the schematic
is what lies.

- `sw-tact` ties pins 1+2 → signal, 3+4 → row (`sw-tact.tsx` TR_p1..p4),
  betting on the {1,2}/{3,4} internal pairing. `BLOCK.md` flagged this exact
  bet: *"if the real pairing is 1+3/2+4 … every board using it is scrap."*
- LCSC's own symbol for C318884 (EasyEDA API, fetched 2026-08-15) draws
  internal bars joining 1–2 and 3–4, with the actuator between the rows —
  the pairing our block assumed. Copper: correct under that pairing.
- But the KiCad export renders the 4-pin button as a **2-pin symbol**, so the
  redundant same-net tie becomes a visible short across the switch. The EE
  reviewed the artifact honestly; the artifact misrepresents the board.

**Generic defect class: a review artifact that misrepresents the design.**
A reviewer can only see what the export shows. If the schematic lies, expert
review is spent debunking phantoms — or worse, trusting phantoms. This is the
same family as the sidecar-vs-rebuild gap: the artifact of record must be
derived truthfully or it is worse than nothing.

**Structural fixes:**
1. Schematic conversion must render the pushbutton with its true pin count
   and internal pairing (or fold redundant same-net ties into the symbol
   instead of drawing them as wires).
2. `sw-tact` should wire **diagonally** (signal → 1, to → 4) so the block is
   correct under either side-pairing and the redundancy bet disappears.
3. First-article continuity check stays mandatory (`BLOCK.md` already says
   so) — the LCSC symbol is strong evidence, not a measurement.

## Finding 2 — routing is "better than before, still messy"

Known; matches our own measurements (router at convergence ceiling,
`pipeline-v2.md`). No new action beyond v2's route stage.

## Finding 3 — USB D+/D− must be a differential pair

**Generic class:** constraint-class routing. D+/D− must run parallel, constant
gap, over a ground reference. Our router has no concept of net classes at all
— this is `docs/lessons.md` open item #12 (pair skew), now confirmed by an
expert as a blocker class, not a nicety.

**What landed (2026-08-16).** The pair is *routed* as a pair, in the pipeline,
on every board — `circuitpy/diffpair.py`, stage 0c. It cannot run before the
autorouter, and the reason is worth recording because it looks like it should:
tscircuit's pre-route hook (`pcbPath`) does pin a trace's copper, but the
subcircuit autorouter's obstacle set is built from components, pads, plated
holes, holes, vias and cutouts — **`pcb_trace` is not in it** — so copper laid
down first is copper the autorouter routes straight through. So the pass runs
*after*, in the space the pair's own detour frees, and refuses rather than
regress.

Measured on hydrate-coaster: coupling **7% → 95%**, skew **13.91mm → 1.95mm**
(inside the 3.8mm USB 2.0 budget), worst clearance **0.115mm → 0.128mm** —
nothing traded, and the pass is graded by the pipeline's own gate rather than
by its own clearance model, because the first version passed its model and
shipped eight hole-clearance errors.

harness-puck and terminal-keyboard refuse: the corridor a pair needs — about a
millimetre of clear copper at the via pitch — does not exist on either. That is
the honest answer, and it is also the argument for moving this stage earlier.

Two things the review's wording caught that our checks had not: the coupling
check matched `DP`/`DM` only at the **end** of a net name, so the
connector-side pair `USB_DP_CONN`/`USB_DM_CONN` was never measured on any
board; and nothing measured the third clause at all — **0% of every board's
USB pair has ground copper on the opposite layer** (`netclass_pair_reference`).

**Still v2's:** reserving the corridor before the autorouter fills it, and a
ground plane under the pair.

## Finding 4 — power traces at signal width

5V and 3V3 run at 0.2mm, same as signals. Works for a low-current keyboard;
wrong as a habit. **We already own this number**: `circuitlib` computes
per-net width from current (ledger #13), but the keyboard predates the fix
and nothing re-checks width *class* — a power net at signal width should warn
even when the current maths squeaks by. **Structural fix:** net-class
minimums in the fab profile (power ≥ 0.5mm default), checked at stage 4.

## Finding 5 — 112×90mm board misses the JLC subsidy by 12mm

Under 100×100mm, 5 sample boards cost **$2**; ours quote **$8.90**. The
planner never knew the price cliff existed. **Generic class: fab economics as
plan-time constraints.** The fab profile knows trace floors but not price
tiers.

*Closed on the board too, 2026-08-16.* The 12mm was never circuit: the switch
pads spanned 97.00mm and all copper 99.00mm, while the M2.5 drills spanned
107.70mm — six screws parked outboard of the keycaps. The board is now
**100 × 90**, the screws moved to the top and bottom rails, and the same
rebuild went from **12 blocking findings to 1**. The printed body changes with
it; the numbers it needs are in `examples/terminal-keyboard/boards/main.tsx`
and the README's *What the printed body has to change*.

**Structural fix:** price-tier table in the fab profile
(`sample_subsidy_max_mm: 100`), a planner target ("fit 100×100 unless the
design cannot"), and a check that names the money when a board exceeds a tier
("12mm over the $2 tier — this board costs 4.5× to sample").

## Finding 6 — BOM/CPL not in JLC's own format; designators not grouped

JLC documents an exact sample format; same-part rows should group
(SW1,SW2,…SW50 = one line). Ours emits one row per part. **Generic class:
export to the consumer's spec, not to what our tools emit naturally.**
**Structural fix:** BOM/CPL writers follow the JLC sample
(jlcpcb.com/help/article/how-to-generate-the-bom-and-centroid-file-from-kicad),
grouped designators, verified by a golden-file test. Applies to every board
the app ever exports.

## Second pass — what changed on 2026-08-16, and what did not

The first pass answered four of the six findings and said so. Re-measuring the
four before showing them to the reviewer again turned up three things the first
pass had recorded wrongly, all of them the same shape: **a value that is absent
makes no claim, so nothing checks it** (ledger lesson F).

**Finding 1 was not fully closed, and we had written the evidence off.** The 50
keys are clean — the KiCad export draws no wire across any of them, verified on
the shipped zip. But `rp2040-core` wires BOOTSEL and RESET inline, and one of
those lines is literally a trace from `SW2.pin2` to `SW2.pin1`: a wire whose two
endpoints are pins of the same switch. `schematic_symbol_short` fired on SW2 and
SW3 on **all three boards, at every rebuild for two days**, and the first pass
dismissed it as the check reading circuit.json rather than the export. Half
right. The KiCad export does fold the tie — and the tscircuit schematic SVG,
which is also an artifact we ship and is the render the reviewer's own
screenshot came from, draws the loop. Fixed by routing both ties through a
named net instead of pin to pin: identical copper, identical connectivity, and
nothing left for a renderer to misread. **The general rule now in the ledger: a
same-net tie between two pins of one component is expressed as a net, never as
a pin-to-pin trace** (#44).

**Finding 6's format was right and its content was empty.** The Footprint
column has been blank on every BOM this project has ever shipped, and the
reason had nothing to do with the exporter: the parts-catalog resolver used one
hard-coded `parents[4]` hop, which is correct in the repo and points at
`skills/packages/parts-catalog/` — a directory that has never existed — in the
runtime that actually builds boards. It returned an empty catalog, and the
function's own docstring called a blank column "the honest answer". Blank is
honest when it means *unknown*; here it meant *we looked in the wrong place*.
The catalog has the packages. Now the root is searched rather than computed,
and a missing one emits `bom_catalog_missing` naming the consequence, because a
blank column nobody is told about is a wrong answer wearing an empty cell.
Two smaller things on the same file went with it: TP1–TP3 no longer ship as
three unbuyable BOM lines, and CPL rotations no longer print as
`202.49999999999994` (#43).

**Finding 4 is now acted on, not only reported.** `circuitpy.powerwidth`
(stage 0d) takes the gap the autorouter already left, segment by segment,
without ever moving a trace — for each piece of copper the widest legal width
is exact and needs no search. It taught us two things worth keeping: a route
point's width is shared by the two segments meeting at it, so one tight pass
necks an entire run unless long segments are cut (they are, into ≤1mm pieces,
then merged back where the widths match); and **a widener must be explicitly
told never to narrow** — the first version let a piece take its computed
allowance even below the copper already there and produced GND at 0.05mm, under
the fab's 0.1mm minimum. Every piece is now floored at the width it started
with, and a test pins it.

Say the limit plainly to the reviewer: **the narrowest point on each rail does
not move.** Roughly half of each ground run now carries 0.5mm instead of 0.2mm,
which is a real reduction in drop, but every rail still necks to signal width
somewhere, and `dfm_power_trace_width` — which reports the minimum — still
fires on all three boards. Closing it properly means the router applying
net-class widths while it routes, so the rail claims its copper and the
neighbours go around, rather than the reverse.

**Finding 3 is unfixed on two of three boards, and the reason we gave for that
was wrong.** `diffpair.py` refused because no channel wide enough survives the
autorouter, and explained that nothing could be reserved beforehand: the
obstacle set was read as components, pads, holes, vias and cutouts, with
`pcb_trace` absent. The `pcb_trace` half is true. The conclusion is not — that
list is a pre-filter, and the obstacle builder itself has an explicit
`pcb_keepout` branch pushing an oval or a rect with `connectedTo: []`. **A
keepout is an obstacle the autorouter honours and it is not copper**, so a
reserved channel costs nothing in the output, and the pair's endpoints are
fixed at placement time, so it costs no extra build either. What was filed as a
research problem is one element. Corrected in the file that got it wrong, and
open as #45.

**Finding 2 now has numbers, and they say something we did not expect.** Until
today "messy" was the one finding we had no instrument for — the reply was
"known, matches our own measurements" and nothing more. There are now six
measures of tangle (`routerlib.quality`), and on the nets both routers
connected, across 12 boards whose placement matches byte for byte:

| | our best (`maze-astar`) | the shipped autorouter |
|---|---|---|
| detour ratio (routed / MST) | **1.06** | 1.32 |
| bends | **595** | 2139 |
| vias | **182** | 360 |
| loop area mm² | **5357** | 6832 |

**The copper the reviewer called tangled is the incumbent's** — `CIRCUIT_ROUTER`
is off by default, so every board he has seen was routed by the right-hand
column. The metrics agree with him about exactly that copper, and they say our
own router is a third less roundabout with a quarter of the bends. Tangle is no
longer a reason to prefer the incumbent; **completeness still is**, and that is
the honest reason the switch is not thrown yet.

Two cautions on the number. It is a *new baseline*, not an improvement — every
score carries quality ruler `b7029fbf41df`, and there is nothing to compare it
against yet. And two measures the brief expected to lead with, mean return
distance and referenced fraction, turned out **flat across every router**: on a
two-layer board with no pour the return path belongs to the board, not to the
router. They are kept as board diagnostics and explicitly barred from any
router objective, because optimising a number the router cannot move is worse
than not measuring it. That finding is the same one as the pour below, arrived
at from the other direction.

**And finding 2 also got the change nobody had asked for explicitly.**
terminal-keyboard and harness-puck had **no ground pour at all** — only
hydrate-coaster did. That single absence is three of the reviewer's findings
wearing different clothes: ground returned as 0.2mm track instead of a plane
(finding 4), the USB pair with 0% reference (finding 3's third clause), and
copper that reads as tangled because every return path is one more line
competing for the same channels (finding 2). Both boards now pour ground on the
bottom layer.

## What this review proves about the process

1. **The two-bar doctrine is real.** `fab.ready=true` and "would not work"
   coexisted on one board. Manufacturable ≠ correct, exactly as written.
2. **Expert review catches the class no check can.** Finding 1 is invisible
   to compile/DRC/DFM by construction — every representation inherits the
   same source. One expert, one look, found it. The review-packet loop is
   load-bearing; it stays.
3. **The block doctrine worked even while its assumption was wrong.** The
   risk was documented in the block, scoped to one file, and fixable in one
   place for every board at once. That is what "fix the class" buys.
