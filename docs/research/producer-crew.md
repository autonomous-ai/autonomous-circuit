# producer-crew

FILM-CREW ORCHESTRATION — RESEARCH → BUILD BRIEF (maps to /Users/d/code/autonomous-tv/docs/film-crew.md)

Grounding: our current film-crew.md has an excellent ROLE roster but is thin on the PROCESS layer — the locks, the handoff artifacts, and the single scheduling authority that make a real production hold quality at speed. Every finding below is a mechanism a great producer/1st AD actually runs, translated into an agent-workflow primitive.

CORE INSIGHT: real productions do NOT run on a "review loop." They run on a small number of HARD LOCKS plus a set of standardized HANDOFF ARTIFACTS that are data contracts between departments. A department is not allowed to start (or spend) until the upstream artifact is locked. This is the single biggest thing to steal — it converts our soft review loop into gated spend and prevents us from scoring/upscaling shots that later get recut.

1) THE TURNOVER / LOCK SEQUENCE. Post has a literal gate: "Once the production team is satisfied with the picture editing, the editing is said to be locked. After this the turnover process begins, in which the picture is prepared for color finishing, and the sound is spotted and turned over to the composer and sound designers." (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-production). So music/SFX/color only begin AFTER picture lock — because the most bespoke, expensive assets (a scored cue, a graded shot) are wasted if the shot gets cut. We currently run color/lip-sync/upscale/audio "in flight" against unlocked material. Fix: encode script-lock → per-shot-accept → picture-lock as real pipeline states that block downstream spend.

2) THE 1st AD IS A BATCHING OPTIMIZER, NOT A CALENDAR. The shot list is grouped into "setups" — "clustering shots sharing identical lighting configurations… prevents redundant equipment moves and optimizes crew time" (https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/how-to-make-a-shot-list/); the stripboard groups scenes by location to minimize company moves. Direct analog: group shots that share the same cast/LoRA + world refs + lighting and dispatch them as one batch (load the LoRA/reference set once, render the cluster) instead of shot-by-shot. This is our render plan and it maps 1:1 to our per-accepted-shot economics.

3) THE CALL SHEET = A COMPLETE, SELF-CONTAINED EXECUTION CONTRACT. "The document consolidates scheduling, locations, crew requirements, and notes into one authoritative resource" and is issued once per day so a shot executes without anyone asking questions (https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-call-sheet/). Steal the pattern for the unit of render dispatch: one record per shot bundling cast_ids + pinned LoRA version + world refs + shot size/angle/lens/movement + lighting + voice + emotion + line. That record is both the parallel-dispatch unit AND the reproducibility record (deterministic re-run of any shot).

4) SCRIPT SUPERVISOR = THE EDITOR'S DATA CONTRACT (we're missing this entirely — it's "planned"). The script sup produces a lined script and a facing-page/daily editor log capturing "take duration, actor positions, screen direction, actions performed, lens type… and director's comments on any particular take as to whether it is no good, a hold take (ok, but not perfect), or a print take" and is "the liaison between the director and the editor" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Script_supervisor). Two steals: (a) "circle takes" / print-take selection — generate N draws, mark ONE print take + hold takes, log it, only the print take advances (this IS our accept/regen mechanic, and it gives the editor ranked fallbacks on a recut); (b) emit a structured per-shot continuity log so the editor agent consumes timing/action/on-camera-dialogue data instead of re-deriving it from pixels.

5) DAILIES = REVIEW WHILE THE CONTEXT IS STILL WARM. Dailies are reviewed the same day so directors catch "dirty lens, focus issues, exposure" and continuity problems and can reshoot "while venues and talent are still available" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dailies). Translated: run per-shot QC IMMEDIATELY after each render, not batched at the end — because that's when the cast LoRA + scene context are still loaded and a regen is cheapest. Reason-coded rejects (we already spec these in the Showrunner API's POST /v1/shots/{id}/reject) should fire the auto-regen inline.

6) SPOTTING SESSION = A TIMECODED MUSIC/SOUND CONTRACT, AFTER LOCK. Post confirms sound is "spotted and turned over to the composer" only after picture lock (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-production). A spotting session walks the locked cut and produces spotting notes + a cue sheet: timestamped in/out points for each music cue and key SFX with intent ("heartbreak motif," "build to climax"). This should be an explicit stage that drives our Lyria (composer) and sfx agents — they never run before it. This directly de-risks our "audio ceiling" work (tasks #20).

7) SCRIPT BREAKDOWN = STAGE 0 THAT POPULATES OUR CONSISTENCY ANCHORS. Breakdown tags every scene's elements by category — cast, extras, props, wardrobe, makeup/hair, VFX, vehicles, set dressing, SFX, music, special equipment — and "the data feeds into budget and schedule generation" (https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/script-breakdown/). This is exactly how a real crew decides WHAT MUST STAY CONSISTENT. An explicit AD-breakdown stage should emit the list of anchors to lock, feeding cast-book (built) and production-design (planned) before any render.

8) COLOR/DI = SHOT-MATCH TO ONE LOOK. DI does a primary grade across the whole frame then secondary grades to "isolate a range of hue/saturation/brightness" on outliers (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_grading) — i.e. establish one look, then conform every shot to it. Our film-crew.md already names this the "single biggest 'looks amateur' tell after character drift." Gen models drift shot-to-shot in color/exposure; a colorist stage that sets one LUT/reference look per episode and shot-matches outliers is a direct fix.

9) DECISION RIGHTS ARE FORMALLY SPLIT — steal it as agent autonomy tiers. The producer "gives final approval on hiring, cast, locations, shooting script, schedule and budget" and is "ultimately financially responsible," while "the director makes the creative decisions during production… within the producer's approved framework" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_producer). Map: Director agent owns creative intent (tone, coverage, performance, print-take); Producer orchestration owns budget/schedule/delivery and cannot override intent; department agents execute strictly within the locked brief and cannot silently re-interpret it; only true creative forks escalate to the human non-producer. This is the org thesis (managers decide, ICs execute, escalate only forks) expressed as a production hierarchy.

10) TEST-SCREENING AUTHORITY = A RETENTION GATE. Producers can "demand additional scenes be filmed" and "request alternative endings based on test screenings" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_producer). Duanju are engineered with a cliffhanger "at the end of nearly every episode," shot in <2 weeks, 20-100 episodes of 1-2 min, cast+locations the top costs, with AI-assisted production and mobile-game-style engagement optimization (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_drama). Steal: an episode-level hook/cliffhanger gate before delivery that scores the first-3-seconds hook and the end-cliffhanger against retention heuristics and can trigger a re-cut or alt opening.

11) EDIT IS STAGED, NOT ONE-SHOT. Post goes assembly → director's cut → picture lock (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-production). Our editor "stitches" in one pass. Splitting rough-assembly (ordered accepted shots, no polish) → director-agent notes → lock lets notes land before any expensive finishing.

WHERE film-crew.md IS ALREADY RIGHT: the skill-vs-engine-stage rule (decision vs execution), consistency anchors as the superpower, and "only the directed department re-runs." The gap is purely the process spine — locks, artifacts, and the AD-as-batcher. The steals below add that spine.
